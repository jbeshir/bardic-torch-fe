The goal of Bardic Torch is to make it as easy as possible for anyone to contribute information – so that instead of thousands of songbooks/indexes/playlists/resources scattered across the internet, we can create one comprehensive index for the bardic community.

Please log in if you have anything to contribute. You do not have to be a bard.

Feel free to [contact us](/contact) if you have a problem.

## Guidelines for contribution

### 1. Any contribution, no matter how small, is appreciated.

In general, it’s completely fine if your contribution is minimal. You can create a page for a bard even if you only know one song of theirs, or create a page for a song even if you don’t know the lyrics. Somebody else can help by adding more information later. The most important thing is that you correctly link and categorise the pages, so that other people can find your work later and build upon it. Make sure you add as many categories as should apply.

It can become difficult to find information in the bardic community when websites go offline, resources disappear or get taken down, and links go dead. Some pieces are probably lost forever because the paper is lost in somebody’s attic and the MySpace or LiveJournal page went down. Our philosophy is therefore to have as many links as possible. Even if there are already four websites hosting the lyrics of a piece, it’s still a useful contribution to add a fifth or sixth link. Even if we already have links to performances on Spotify or YouTube, it’s still helpful to add links to performances on Bandcamp or SoundCloud. You never know when a website may go down.

### 2. Do not upload private or personal information to Bardic Torch.

Stick to what bards have published on album pages, blogs and websites – do not cite private/personal social media posts. A public-facing social media page under a public name is ok, but no posts from private groups.

### 3. Do not upload copyrighted information.

Please do not upload copyrighted information to Bardic Torch. Link to performances and lyrics elsewhere – do not host them here.

Bardic Torch never hosts lyrics, even if the song is released through Creative Commons or the public domain, and there is no way to upload files (images, videos).

### 4. Provide citations for information about licensing and etiquette.

We cannot verify who edited a Bardic Torch page, so you can’t edit a page to say “I’m the author and I give permission for you to sing this song”. You need to post that information from your own social media account, or on your own page or blog or website. Then you can link to that citation.

If we allow “I heard third-hand around the campfire that this bard gives everyone permission to sell her music”, there is an unacceptably high risk of our site encouraging and enabling copyright violations. Please encourage bards to talk about their preferences, licensing and permissions online.

## How editing works

Log in with Google or Facebook, then choose **Edit** on any page, or **Add a piece** or **Add a bard** on the [pieces](/pieces) and [bards](/bards) lists. Fill in what you know, use **Preview** to check it, and save with a few words on what you changed.

Every edit from a contributor is checked by a moderator before it goes live. Until then you see your own version, with a banner saying it is waiting for review; everyone else sees the last approved version. Moderators’ own edits go live straight away. Every page keeps a public history.

## I want to create a page for a bard

First check to see if a page for the bard already exists by looking for them in [Bards](/bards). They may be listed under another name than the name you are expecting; check their legal name, stage name, SCA persona name, etc.

If a page does not exist, choose **Add a bard** and fill out the form with all the information you have.

## I want to create a page for a piece

First, check to see if a page for the piece already exists. Look in [Pieces](/pieces) to see if the piece is there, either under the title you know it by or under another title. You may also look at the bard’s page to see if it is listed under their works.

Try searching for the elements in the title which are shared between multiple versions. (For example, if a piece is sometimes known as “The Daring Exploits of Knight McKnight”, sometimes as “Daring Exploits of Knight McKnight” and sometimes simply as “Knight McKnight”, try searching just for “Knight McKnight”. That way, it should show up even if the page isn’t named what you expect.) If you know a song by a different title, you don’t have to create a separate page for it; add your title to the existing page as another title, so that anyone who searches for “The Daring Exploits of Knight McKnight” finds the existing page for “Knight McKnight”.

You can use the search bar at the top of the site to search for the song’s title.

If the song doesn’t already have a page, congrats! You’ve found a piece that will be very useful to contribute to Bardic Torch!

Choose **Add a piece**, choose what kind of piece it is, and fill out the form with all the information you have. It is absolutely fine to simply create the page, add the information you know, and leave it for somebody else to update later. Even if you do nothing else, that’s helpful! Other editors can come along later and see that information needs to be added to the song’s page. Telling us that a song exists by this name tells us where to search.

### Authors

Add the name(s) of the authors: who wrote the words and who wrote the tune (or, for a poem or story, the author). If the bard already has a page, please try to use the same name as their page uses. For example, if Bard McBard’s real name is Jane Smith, but she publishes her work under her SCA name of Bard McBard, use “Bard McBard” so that the links direct users to her bard page. If the bard does not have a page, try to use the name that they most often publish/share their work under. A bard who doesn’t have a page yet gets a short stub page, which anyone can help complete.

### Key links

“Key Links” exists to help highlight links that are especially important to a song. For example, if the author themselves has published the song on their own blog, or the author has an official page where you can buy the album, that is the ‘canonical’ link to the song. Users might want to be able to find it more easily than they find third-party lyrics pages or third-party performances.

It’s okay to leave this blank if you don’t have any key links for this song.

### Lyrics

Please don’t add lyrics directly to Bardic Torch. Instead, add links to where the lyrics can be found elsewhere.

If you can’t find a page that has the lyrics, it’s okay to leave this blank.

### Licensing & etiquette

This is where you can add any information like “the bard says it is okay for anyone to perform this song so long as it’s for free” or “the bard requests that this song is never written down and only shared through oral tradition” or “the bard released this song into the public domain” or “the bard asks that you get her permission before performing this song, if she’s at the event”. You should link to the webpage, blog post, social media post, video, etc where the bard shared this information.

**Important: You must have a citation for this information, even if you are the bard.** We cannot verify that you are the bard from your Bardic Torch account. _Anyone_ could edit a page to say “the bard is okay with people selling her work” – and we can’t risk someone adding that kind of information erroneously or maliciously, as it would make us responsible for copyright violations. Please post this information from your personal account or on your personal webpage, and then **cite** that page when you add information to Bardic Torch.

### Performances & recordings

You cannot add performances or recordings directly to Bardic Torch; you must link to resources elsewhere. If you can’t find a performance or recording online, it’s totally fine to leave this blank.

Please include as much information as you can about the performance/recording. Who is performing the piece? Where? When? Who uploaded it to the internet? What website are you linking to? For example: “Performance on YouTube, uploaded by So-and-so, of the song performed by Such-and-such at Event Name in 2026”. This will really help people find the piece again if, in the future, links go dead or websites go down.

## I want to help improve existing pages

Extremely helpful activities include:

- Finding more performances, and linking to those performances on both the piece’s page and the bard’s page. When you add a performance to a piece page, if you also credit the bard as a performer, that’s even more helpful.
- Completing stub pages (pages that should have more, but don’t yet). Look at other, similar pages to see how the page should be set up. See [pages that need work](/needs-work).
- Adding links and categories to pages that ought to be properly interlinked and categorised, but aren’t yet. Check whether categories have sub-categories or should be sorted into overarching sur-categories, and so on.
- Finding information about licensing, permissions, bards’ preferences, etc and linking it on both piece pages and bard pages.

## Categories & tagging guide

Bardic Torch uses categories to organise songs, singers, stories, storytellers, poems, poets, fools, dances, bands, choirs, troupes, and all other sorts of bardic material. Please follow these guidelines to keep everything consistent and searchable. See [all categories](/categories).

### Categories for pieces

Categories are what allow users to search pieces on Bardic Torch for pieces that they want to perform. Try to add as many as you can, but don’t worry if you don’t know how to use all of them. It is totally okay to leave some fields blank. Another editor can add the information later. You only have to contribute what you know and are comfortable contributing.

- **Words** should credit the lyricist, and **Tune** the composer; for a poem or story, **Author** should credit the author.
- Choose one or more **Mood** categories
- Choose one or more **Topic** categories
- Choose one or more **Culture** categories
- There should generally be only one **Source Category** (called **Origin** on Bardic Torch)
- You may add additional categories under **Other**

Each documentable historical piece should also link its **source** (as a text source) and give its **century** (a century category under Origin).

### Genre

On Bardic Torch, a piece’s genre is its kind. Choose **one**:

- **Song** should be used for all musical pieces that have lyrics that can be sung.
- **Instrumental** should be used for all musical pieces that have sheet music available for instrumentalists. A musical piece can be tagged as both Song and Instrumental if it can be performed by both singers and instrumentalists: choose **Song**, and add the **Instrumental** tag under [Other](#other-categories).
- **Story** should be used for all narrative spoken pieces, regardless of format or tradition or length.
- **Poem** should be used for all poems that are **not** set to music. Please do not tag songs as poems, even if the lyrics rhyme.
- **Dance & music** should be used for dance performances.

Comedy (fooling and foolery, standup comedy, jester acts, clowning or similar work) is not a genre on Bardic Torch: comedic pieces use the **Comedic** [mood](#mood).

Additional genres may be added later for work that truly cannot be categorised under the existing scheme, but please keep the number of tags limited.

### Mood

Bardic Torch uses a **limited** number of mood tags to help ensure work is findable and the tag system remains manageable. The intent of a ‘mood’ tag is to help bards find repertoire that will work in a particular context: a performer at a party or a bar may be seeking happy or funny pieces and wishing to avoid tragic pieces, while a performer at a funeral may have the opposite preference. A song’s mood does not have to be perfectly captured by the tag, so long as it would be appropriate to perform in similar contexts to the other songs in the tag.

- **Tragic** should be used for all pieces that are sad, mournful, tragic, wistful, solemn, or otherwise suitable for more sombre occasions; sad song contests, funerals, etc.
- **Comedic** should be used for all pieces that are funny, silly, comic or humorous; suitable for parties and taverns, unsuitable for sombre occasions.
- **Joyful** should be used for all pieces that are happy, victorious, triumphant, upbeat, ‘party’ music or otherwise suitable for celebrations.
- **Warlike** should be used for pieces that are suitable for performance on a re-enactment battlefield or in relation to martial arts activities; often these will be war-related, angry, determined, fierce or upbeat.
- **Relaxed** should be used for pieces that are calm, lullabies, sweet, soft, soothing and otherwise suitable for a late-night quiet campfire or for sleepy audiences.
- **Sentimental** should be used for pieces that are highly emotional, but don’t fit into the strictly-happy Joyful or strictly-sad Tragic categories; those that are wistful, nostalgic, sweet, inspiring, uplifting, persuasive, longing or philosophical.
- **Dance Music** should be used for musical pieces that a bard may wish to perform at a dance, for dancers. They can be happy or sad, but should be pieces that a dance has been performed to. Distinct from Dance & music pieces, which are the dances themselves.
- **Story (Mood)** should be used when a piece tells a story too complex to categorise as happy or sad, because both kinds of events happen. It should be used for pieces that have a distinct plot arc.
- **Romantic** should be used for music that is romantic; it may be suitable for weddings, or for performing to a romantic interest as part of flirting, or it may include praise pieces about the performer’s partner/spouse/inspiration.
- **Singalong** should be used for songs that are often used as group singalong songs. They typically have very easy melodies, upbeat tempo, simple well-known lyrics, actions and/or call-and-response sections.
- **Eerie** should be used for songs that are scary, eerie, creepy, sinister, ghostly, chilling, fear-inducing or unsettling; suitable for Halloween celebrations, spooky song contests, or to perform alongside ghost stories.
- **Rude** – Use for works that contain bawdy, profane or blasphemous content that might be inappropriate to perform in front of children or polite company.

Try to use one of these broad categories as a piece’s mood. It is possible to create more specific categories, but remember that the ‘mood’ tag is intended to help bards find pieces _appropriate for a particular context or situation_. We do not intend to split the ‘Joyful’ tag into ‘Excited’, ‘Happy’, ‘Cheerful’ or other extremely subjective sub-categories. You might, however, create a new category under ‘Warlike’ for songs suitable as marching songs, or a new category under ‘Tragic’ for songs specifically suitable at funerals/remembrances, or a new category under ‘Romantic’ for songs appropriate at weddings.

### Topic

You can choose multiple topics. Choose only the main topics of the song, not every item mentioned in the song.

Keep topics broad — avoid overly specific tags. If it can be filed under an existing tag, use the existing tag. Create a new tag only if the topic cannot be reasonably filed under existing tags.

Use sub-categories for more specific topics wherever possible. For example, ‘Swords’, ‘Battles’ or ‘Shieldwalls’ should all be sub-categories of ‘War’ and not main topic headings.

### Culture

Use broad cultural categories where possible.

The intent of Culture categories is not to categorise the bards’ backgrounds, the language of pieces, or necessarily to correctly identify the origin culture of the piece. It is to help performers and audiences identify suitable repertoire for the persona or setting they are portraying in re-enactment or LARP – for example, to help someone with a Viking SCA persona find suitable pieces for Norse-themed performance, or to help someone find appropriate French or France-related pieces to perform at a LARP set in Paris.

Multiple Culture tags may apply to a piece. Culture tags can be applied based on the documentable origin of a work, the style of instrumentation or melody, mentions of cultural elements (eg. song lyrics about gods from a particular pantheon) or historical events, or literal description of a culture or place in the work’s text.

When listing multiple broader and narrower Culture tags, move from broad to specific – eg. “European, British, English”

### Source category (Origin)

Bardic pieces come from many places; historically documentable pieces, original pieces written by members of modern bardic communities (including the SCA, LARP, SF&F, historical re-enactment and more), folk music, music from historical and fantasy fiction, filk, and more. The ‘source category’ field attempts to categorise works by their broad origin, whether someone dug them up from a twelfth-century manuscript or composed them at a Lord of the Rings convention.

**Important:** Please do not use “filk”. The term “filk” has many different meanings in different communities; some commonly use it to refer to SF&F songs, while others use it exclusively for contrafacta and others use it for all ‘nerd music’. We have created specific tags with clearer, agreed-upon meanings.

Choose **one**:

- **Pre-1600 Sources** for pieces that are **documentably** pre-1600 in origin.
- **Traditional & Folk** for pieces that are not documentably pre-1600, but are old enough to not be copyrightable (or do not have a known author). Do not use for pieces protected by copyright, even if they are in the folk ‘genre’.
- **Borrowings** for pieces created by modern authors who are **not** part of the bardic tradition, such as modern mundane bands, which may be often performed in a bardic context. Be especially careful of copyright with these pieces.
- **SCA Pieces** for pieces written by bards performing in an SCA context. Do not use for any and all pieces performed in the SCA – only those written by members of the SCA.
- **SF&F Pieces** for pieces relating to SF&F shows, books, movies, and other fandom.
- **Contrafacta** for pieces that set new words to an existing, historical tune – also called “parody”, “filk”, “lyrical adaptation”, “rewrite” and so on. Use ‘contrafacta’ first, but you may also categorise these pieces as pre-1600 (for documentably pre-1600 melodies) or traditional (for folk tunes), and may categorise them as SCA or SF&F if the new words were written by SCA or SF&F-related artists. If the piece sets new words to an existing modern tune that is under copyright, use **Contrafacta (Modern)** instead. If the piece sets new words to a tune written by an SCA bard, use **Contrafacta (SCA)** instead.
- **Setting Pieces** for pieces that set old words to a new tune – taking a historical poem or text and creating a new original melody for it. Use ‘Setting Pieces’ first, but you may also categorise these pieces as pre-1600 or traditional if the words are pre-1600 or traditional, and may categorise them as SCA or as Borrowings if the melody is by a contemporary composer.

Use **only** the categories from the list above as a piece’s main source category; all pieces should be categorised as one of the above. If you wish to create other categories or source categories on this theme, place them under Origin, after the main one.

### Other categories

Optional tags for special cases:

- **Instrumental** – Use for pieces of another genre, such as songs, that also have sheet music available for instrumentalists (see [Genre](#genre)).
- **True Events** – Use for pieces that describe events that really happened (or are claimed to have really happened) within bardic or associated communities (eg. the SCA, re-enactment groups, LARP groups). Do not use for historical events!
- **Long Pieces** – Use for pieces that exceed the limit of what can normally be performed at a bardic circle – 5+ minutes for songs, 10+ minutes for stories or epic poetry.
- **Short Pieces** – use for pieces that are unusually short (eg. one-minute stories, one-verse songs or one-stanza poems).
- See **Languages** for languages other than English.
- See **Legendaria** for pieces relevant to particular literary collections of legends and myths – eg. Arthuriana, the Fenian/Ossianic Cycle, Saints’ lives.

### Creating categories & subcategories

If you don’t see an existing category that suits your needs, you can create a new category. This is very easy. Every new category needs a parent: if you make a new top-level topic, put it under Topics; if you make a new sub-topic, put it under its topic. For example, Heraldry is a subcategory of SCA (Topic). A moderator checks a new category before pieces can use it.

It is important that categories are all named in the same style. Please name categories in the same style of natural English, capitalising content words. Avoid punctuation other than `()`, `'` and `&`. A new category for ‘Green Eggs and Ham’ should be named **Green Eggs & Ham**, not GreenEggs&Ham, GreenEggsAndHam, green eggs n ham, Green eggs And Ham, “Green Eggs and Ham”, Green Eggs/Ham, or any other variation.

### Categories for bards

Bard pages are titled according to the name the bard presents themselves with online. Use the name they publish under – whether this be a legal name, SCA name, stage name, or nickname – and then add other names as the bard’s other names.

List affiliations from broad to specific, eg. “SCA – Kingdom Name – Household Name”. Affiliations include SCA and Amtgard.

Performer categories: Musicians (Singers, Instrumentalists, Songwriters), Storytellers, Jesters (use for comics, fools, jokers, etc), Poets and Dancers.
